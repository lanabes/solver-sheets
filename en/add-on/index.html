The Solver add-on for Google Sheets

Excel’s Solver, inside any Google Sheets spreadsheet: pick the objective cell, the changing cells and the limits, and click “Solve”. It reads your own formulas as they are, creates answer and sensitivity reports, and comes with Goal Seek too.

In short

  • Free up to 200 variables and 500 constraints, no daily solve limit, no sign-up.
  • Excel-style reports: answer and sensitivity (shadow prices, reduced costs and ranges), with a plain-English reading.
  • Your data never reaches us: it runs in your Google account and only touches the open spreadsheet.

What it does

It finds the values that give the best possible result (more profit, less cost, fewer hours) without breaking the limits you set. Use it for production mixes, transport, staff scheduling, budgets and any split of a resource that can’t cover everything. If you’re not sure how to set up a problem, the Solver guide walks you through it.

  • Works on your sheet as it is. No need to copy the model into a special layout: it reads the objective and constraints from your own formulas (SUMPRODUCT, SUM, sums and products by numbers, references across tabs).
  • Continuous, integer and binary variables (yes/no), with the “Make unconstrained variables non-negative” checkbox, as in Excel.
  • Max, min or a specific value for the objective cell.
  • Explains why it can’t: if there’s no solution, if the objective can grow forever or if a formula isn’t linear, it tells you which cell and leaves the sheet as it was.
  • One-click Undo puts the previous values back.
  • Goal Seek included, for when you just want one formula to hit a value by changing one cell.
  • Remembers the model for each spreadsheet: close it, come back, it’s still there.

Answer and sensitivity reports

After solving, two buttons create each report in a new tab, with the same columns as Excel’s:

ReportWhat it tells you
AnswerThe objective and each changing cell before and after, and every constraint with its final value, whether it’s binding, and its slack.
SensitivityEach constraint’s shadow price (how much one more unit improves the objective) and how far it holds; each variable’s reduced cost and how far its coefficient can move without changing the solution.

Under the tables, a reading in plain sentences. For the workshop example in the guide: “Each extra unit of ‘Lathe hours’ changes the objective by 10, as long as it doesn’t rise by more than 20.” Every cell shows up with its name, taken from the labels in its row and column, as in Excel.

As in Excel, sensitivity only exists when every variable is continuous: with integer or binary variables, the add-on creates the answer report and explains why there’s no sensitivity.

How to use it

  1. Open the sidebar. In your spreadsheet, ExtensionsSolverSolver…. The first time, Google asks for permission: the add-on can only see and edit the spreadsheet you have open and show its sidebar.
  2. Objective cell. The one with the formula you want to maximize or minimize (or bring to a value). Select it in the sheet and click the selection button next to the field.
  3. Changing cells. Your decisions: how many units, how many people, how much money. They must be numbers typed by hand or empty. Choose whether they’re continuous, integer or binary.
  4. Constraints. A range, the sign and the limit: for example D9:D11 ≤ E9:E11 compares three rows at once. The limit can be a number or a cell.
  5. Solve. The add-on writes the solution into your cells and shows the objective value, which constraints are binding and the report buttons. Not convinced? “Undo”.

First time? In the sidebar, “Insert a worked example” creates a tab with the workshop from the guide, ready to go. Click “Solve” and you’ll see the answer (2,600) in a second.

Limits and good practice

  • Up to 200 changing cells and 500 constraints (each cell of a range counts as one).
  • To read your model, the add-on changes the cells one at a time and watches your formulas respond: it takes about a second per changing cell. When it finishes, the sheet is either as it was or holds the solution.
  • The model must be linear: no multiplying two changing cells together, and no IF, MAX or powers that depend on them.
  • If the sheet has random formulas (RAND) or formulas that depend on the time, the answer may not be stable.

Frequently asked questions

How much does the Solver add-on cost?

Today it’s free, with no sign-up and no daily solve limit, for models of up to 200 variables and 500 constraints. If we ever add paid options, we’ll say so on this page first, and nothing will ever be charged unless you buy it.

I get “access denied” when granting permission. What do I do?

Google shows each permission with its own checkbox, and if they aren’t all ticked, it denies access. Open Extensions → Solver → Solver… again and, in Google’s window, tick both boxes: see and edit the spreadsheet you use it in, and show its sidebar. The add-on can’t work without either of them.

Does my data leave my spreadsheet?

It never reaches us. The add-on runs inside your Google account, can only read and write the spreadsheet you have open, and solves with the optimization service built into Google. It uses no servers of its own and has no permission to connect anywhere else.

Does it solve nonlinear models, like Excel’s GRG?

No. It solves linear models with continuous, integer or binary variables: the equivalent of Excel’s “Simplex LP” method. If a formula multiplies two changing cells, it tells you which one instead of returning a wrong answer.

What is the sensitivity report?

The same as Excel’s: for each constraint, how much the objective would improve with one more unit of the resource (the shadow price) and how far that holds; for each variable, how far its coefficient can move without changing the solution. The add-on adds a plain-English reading underneath. Like in Excel, it only exists when every variable is continuous.

How is it different from the free template?

The template has its own table layout: you copy the sheet and type your problem into it. The add-on works on any sheet of yours, with your model as it is and your own formulas, like Excel’s Solver.

Does it work with OpenSolver or Excel’s Solver?

It’s independent of both. If you come from Excel, the sidebar will feel familiar: objective cell, max, min or value, changing cells and constraints. If OpenSolver keeps failing, see why that happens.

The answer is only as good as the model. If a constraint is missing or a number is wrong, the solution may be impossible to carry out. Always review it before you decide anything with it.
